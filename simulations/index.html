Simulations

Interacting particle systems and percolation models are easiest to get a feeling for by watching them. The two labs below run live in your browser: one on the long-time behaviour of non-equilibrium dynamics, where rotating and oscillating states appear, and one on percolation in the k-neighbour graphs, where every vertex opens a fixed number of edges. Open a lab to start it.

Interacting particle systems · non-equilibrium dynamics Driven clock lab Driven clock and XY models in 2D and 3D: rotating states, spiral defects and long-range correlations, and Lipowski's oscillating prey–predator lattice.

Every spin is a clock hand that interacts with its neighbours like an XY spin and is pushed to rotate by a constant drift: the driven clock and XY models of C. Maes and S. Shlosman, Rotating states in driven clock- and XY-models, J. Stat. Phys. 144 (2011). In two dimensions the collective rotation is destroyed by spiral defects; in three dimensions the whole system rotates coherently. The pictures follow the conventions of Y. Avni, M. Fruchart, D. Martin, D. Seara and V. Vitelli, Dynamical phase transitions in the nonreciprocal Ising model, Phys. Rev. E 111 (2025), whose nonreciprocal Ising model is closely related to the four-state clock. A second model, A. Lipowski's lattice of prey and predators, Phys. Rev. E 60 (1999), oscillates for a different reason: in two dimensions only the noise has a preferred period, in three dimensions the densities oscillate coherently.

Pick a preset or change the parameters yourself; β and d take effect immediately. The diagnostics below the lattice views measure truncated correlations in space and in time and compare them with the spin-wave predictions (for the prey–predator model also the power spectrum of the prey density); the notes below sketch the phase diagram conjectured by Maes and Shlosman.

Truncated correlations in space, at a fixed time t

Ct(r) = ⟨u0(t)·ure(t)⟩ − ⟨u0(t)⟩·⟨ure(t)⟩ for the local state u = (prey present, predator present) ∈ {0, 1}², estimated from the configuration at time t by averaging over all sites and the d lattice directions e. In the oscillating phase the local phase of the oscillation is a soft mode, as the phase of the magnetisation is in the rotating clock, and Ct(r) should show the same spin-wave decay |x|2−d.

Ct(r) = ⟨σ0(t)·σre(t)⟩ − ⟨σ0(t)⟩·⟨σre(t)⟩ with σ = (cos θ, sin θ), estimated from the configuration at time t by averaging over all sites and the d lattice directions e.

current time t mean over the last 0 times mean, finite-size corrected spin waves on the L-torus, A·GL(r) A·G∞(r) ≈ A/(4πr), the decay |x|2−d

On the torus, subtracting the magnetization (for the prey–predator model: the densities) removes the k = 0 spin wave, which bends Ct(r) down long before r = L/2. The rings add back exactly this spin-wave finite-size effect, A·[G∞(r) − GL(r)], so in 3D they should lie on the 1/r line out to r ≈ L/2. The amplitude A is fitted for r ≤ L/4. Let the run relax for a few hundred time units: the longest spin waves equilibrate on a time scale of order L².

Truncated correlations in time

S(t0, t) = L−d Σx ux(t0)·ux(t) − U(t0)·U(t), with u = (prey, predator) and U = (x, y) the densities, as in Chaté, Grinstein and Tang. It oscillates with the population cycle; its envelope is predicted to decay like τ−(d−2)/2, that is τ−1/2 in d = 3.

S(t0, t) = L−d Σx σx(t0)·σx(t) − M(t0)·M(t), as in Chaté, Grinstein and Tang. It oscillates with the collective rotation; its envelope is predicted to decay like |t − t0|−(d−2)/2, that is t−1/2 in d = 3.

|S(τ)|, the oscillating autocorrelation co-rotating Srot(τ), its envelope spin waves on the L-torus, B·HL(D τ) ∝ τ−(d−2)/2 = τ−1/2

For the co-moving Srot, the deviation ux − U is split into its component along the velocity V of U(t) on the limit cycle, scaled by |V|rms/|V| so that it measures a shift of the local phase, and the normal component. This removes the oscillation and leaves the envelope; the curve B·HL(D τ) is the same spin-wave autocorrelation as for the clock, with a fitted phase diffusion constant D.

Srot measures every spin relative to the current direction of M, which removes the oscillation and leaves the envelope: Srot(t0, t) = Re L−d Σx δw̄x(t0) δwx(t), δw = eiθ M̄/|M| − |M|. HL(u) = L−d Σk≠0 e−uλk/λk is the spin-wave autocorrelation on the torus, with a fitted phase diffusion constant D.

A single reference time t0 gives a very ragged curve: S(t0, t) is dominated by the few longest spin waves, whose amplitudes fluctuate strongly. Both curves therefore average S(t0, t0 + τ) over all time origins t0 in a moving window, with exponential weights over the last – time units. Averaging starts 200 time units after a restart, so the relaxation from the initial state does not enter, and starts over when a parameter changes.

Power spectrum of the prey density

S(ω) of x(t), recorded once per Monte Carlo step: an average over Hann-windowed blocks of 256 steps from the last 4096 steps, normalised so that summing over ω gives the variance σ² of x(t). A sharp peak means a regular oscillation, a broad bump only a preferred time scale of the noise.

S(ω) position of the highest peak

Whether the oscillation survives in the infinite lattice is decided by σ·Ld/2: for independent noise it stays of order one as L grows, for a genuine collective oscillation it grows like Ld/2.

How the finite-size corrections are computed

Spin waves on the torus. In the ordered (rotating or oscillating) phase the large-scale fluctuations are those of a phase field φx, the local deviation of the angle θx (or of the phase of the population cycle) from its spatial mean. To leading order the phase is Gaussian with independent Fourier modes, whose variance is inversely proportional to the lattice Laplacian. On the L-torus the wave vectors are k ∈ (2π/L){0,…,L−1}d and

λk = Σi 2 (1 − cos ki), ⟨|φ̂k|²⟩ ∝ 1/λk (k ≠ 0).

Subtracting the spatial mean of the configuration at time t (the magnetisation M(t), or the densities U(t) for the prey–predator model) removes exactly the k = 0 mode. The truncated correlation along a lattice axis is therefore, up to the amplitude A and a short-range part,

Ct(r) ≈ A · GL(r), GL(r) = L−d Σk≠0 cos(k1 r) / λk,

the Green's function of −Δ on the torus with the zero mode removed (dashed line). It is computed exactly: the sum over the d − 1 transverse components is done once for every k1, which leaves a single cosine sum over k1 for every r. GL is negative near r = L/2, because the subtracted mode has to integrate to zero; this is why the measured curves bend down long before r = L/2 and why a plain log-log plot hides the 1/r decay.

The amplitude. A is fitted by least squares, A = Σr C(r) GL(r) / Σr GL(r)², over 1 ≤ r ≤ L/4 (2 ≤ r ≤ L/4 for the prey–predator model, where the local state contributes a sizeable short-range part at r = 1). The fit uses the mean over the last 30 measurements, taken four times per second.

The correction. In infinite volume the same spin waves give A·G∞(r), with the lattice Green's function of Z3 along an axis,

G∞(r) = ∫0∞ e−6s I0(2s)² Ir(2s) ds → 1/(4πr) as r → ∞ (In: modified Bessel functions),

tabulated for r ≤ 48 (G∞(1) = 0.0861, G∞(3) = 0.0275 against 1/(12π) = 0.0265) and replaced by 1/(4πr) beyond, where the two agree to four digits. The rings are the measured mean with the torus spin-wave part exchanged for the infinite-volume one,

Ccorr(r) = C(r) + A · [G∞(r) − GL(r)].

This assumes that the finite size enters only through the long-wavelength spin waves, while the short-range part of C does not depend on L. If the spin-wave picture is right, the rings lie on the dotted line A/(4πr) ∝ |x|2−d out to r ≈ L/2; deviations at large r mean that the run has not relaxed yet, since the longest modes relax on a time scale L²/(4π²D). In d = 2 there is no correction: G∞ diverges and the spin-wave decay is only logarithmic.

In time. Every spin-wave mode relaxes independently, ∂tφ̂k = −D λk φ̂k + noise, so in the co-moving frame (which removes the oscillation of the lab-frame S) the autocorrelation is

Srot(τ) ≈ B · HL(D τ), HL(u) = L−d Σk≠0 e−u λk / λk = ∫u∞ [hL(s)d − L−d] ds, hL(s) = L−1 Σa e−s λa (the one-dimensional return probability on the torus).

HL is computed from this product formula by quadrature on a logarithmic grid. In infinite volume h(s)3 ≈ (4πs)−3/2, which gives H∞(u) ≈ 2(4π)−3/2 u−1/2: the envelope τ−1/2 (dotted line). On the torus the smallest λk is about 4π²/L², so the curve leaves the power law near τ ≈ L²/(4π²D) and then decays exponentially. B and D are fitted to Srot by least squares in the relative error (D on a logarithmic grid, refined by golden-section search, then smoothed over successive fits).

Limitations. The Gaussian spin-wave description ignores the nonlinear (KPZ-type) terms in the phase dynamics of Grinstein, Mukamel, Seidin and Bennett. In d = 3 they renormalise A, B and D but keep the |x|2−d and τ−(d−2)/2 laws; vortex lines and other defects are not part of the description at all.

The model

Each site of the periodic lattice carries a clock spin k ∈ {0,…,q−1} with angle φ = 2πk/q. It advances or retreats by one step at the rates of Maes and Shlosman (Eq. 4), simulated exactly in continuous time:

c±(x) = p± · exp{ (β/2) Σ_y~x [ cos(φx − φy ± 2π/q) − cos(φx − φy) ] } d = log(p+/p−), p+ + p− = 1

For d = 0 the dynamics is reversible with respect to the Gibbs measures of the clock model H = −Σ cos(φx − φy). For d > 0 every free spin turns counterclockwise, and the question is what the interaction makes of this drive. The XY option integrates the limit q → ∞ (Eq. 7), dφ = d·dt − Σ sin(φx − φy) dt + (2/β)½ dW, whose generator commutes with that of the undriven XY model. Its stationary translation-invariant measure is unique for almost all β, yet at low temperature the rotated XY Gibbs states rotate forever and never relax to it (Theorem 7).

Reading the views

Pictures use θ = 2π(k + ½)/q, a global half-step rotation. For q = 4 the four states then sit on the diagonals, so σA = sgn cos θ and σB = sgn sin θ are two Ising spins. Their cycle (++) → (−+) → (−−) → (+−) is the swap order of the nonreciprocal Ising model of Avni et al.. The driven 4-clock is their fully antisymmetric model with exponential instead of Glauber rates, with J̃ = dim·β and K̃ = d/2.

A vortex is a plaquette whose four corners wind once around the clock. In 3D, voxels are drawn semi-transparent with grey encoding depth (dark = front).

The conjectured phase diagram in d ≥ 3

Equilibrium (d = 0, Conjecture 2). For q large the 3D clock model has two transitions. Below βcr the Gibbs state is unique and correlations decay exponentially. Between βcr and βG there are q magnetized phases whose truncated correlations decay only algebraically: the discreteness of the clock is invisible at these scales and the model behaves like the XY model, with massless spin waves (Goldstone modes). Above βG the Pirogov–Sinai theory applies and correlations decay exponentially again. Both βG and the Pirogov–Sinai threshold tend to infinity as q → ∞.

With drift (Conjectures 3–6). In the Pirogov–Sinai regime the q phases survive a small drift d < dcr(β, q): they stay static and close to equilibrium. Above dcr the magnetization rotates, |⟨σx⟩T − m(T)ei(Φ(T)+φk)| → 0 with periodic m and Φ, and the state never converges. In the soft-mode regime βcr < β < βG the critical drift vanishes: any d > 0 sets the spins rotating synchronously. The stationary translation-invariant distribution is then unique with zero magnetization, while the rotating states are not attracted to it.

q = 4. The four-state clock is two Ising models and has no soft-mode phase in equilibrium. For the corresponding fully antisymmetric nonreciprocal Ising model, Avni et al. find numerically that static order is unstable to droplet growth in any finite dimension and that the transition from disorder to the rotating (swap) phase is in the 3D XY universality class. The 3D preset sits just above this transition.

Correlations in the rotating phase. The rotation breaks the continuous time-translation symmetry, and the local phase of the oscillation is a soft mode. Following Grinstein, Mukamel, Seidin and Bennett, its fluctuations obey a KPZ equation whose smooth phase exists for d > 2. This predicts equal-time truncated correlations decaying like |x|2−d and autocorrelations whose envelope decays like |t|−(d−2)/2 (Chaté, Grinstein and Tang). For the driven XY model the spatial law follows from Theorem 7, since the rotating states are rotated equilibrium XY states with spin-wave correlations. The two correlation panels above test both predictions, together with their spin-wave forms on the finite torus.

A second oscillator: Lipowski's prey–predator lattice

The Prey–predator model is the lattice model of A. Lipowski, Oscillatory behavior in a lattice prey-predator system. Every site is empty, holds a prey, a predator, or both. A site is chosen at random; with probability r its prey (if any) breeds, otherwise its predator (if any) acts:

prey if some neighbour has no prey, put a new prey on one of those neighbours (chosen uniformly) predator no prey on its site: the predator dies; otherwise it eats that prey and, if some neighbour has no predator, puts a new predator there

One unit of time is a Monte Carlo step, Ld updates; the start is random with independent densities ½. Unlike the driven clock there is no rotation symmetry and no drift: the oscillation of the densities x (prey) and y (predators) is generated by the dynamics alone, as in the Lotka–Volterra equations. The all-prey state is absorbing, and the active phase ends at rc ≈ 0.11 in 2D and 0.05 in 3D in a directed-percolation transition.

What the paper finds. In 1D the densities only fluctuate. In 2D their power spectrum has a peak, but the amplitude decays like L−d/2, so in the infinite lattice x and y are constant. In 3D, for r near 0.3, x(t) and y(t) oscillate regularly, with a sharp spectral peak and its second harmonic, and the amplitude σ stays finite as L grows. The coherent oscillation is a property of the infinite system, like the rotating state of the driven clock in 3D, although the mean-field equations alone predict a stable fixed point.

Correlations. The analogy goes further. In the oscillating phase the local phase of the population cycle is a soft mode, like the phase of the magnetisation in the rotating clock, and the argument of Grinstein et al. and Chaté, Grinstein and Tang applies to it as well. With the local state u = (prey, predator), the equal-time truncated correlations follow the spin-wave curve A·GL(r) on the torus, so they decay like |x|2−d = 1/r, and the autocorrelation in the frame moving along the limit cycle follows B·HL(Dτ), with envelope τ−1/2, and phase diffusion constant D ≈ 0.1 (L = 48 after 15 000 steps, figure at the end of the page). The correlation panels of the simulator measure both live; because D is small, the longest modes need a few thousand steps to relax.

Checked here. With the same dynamics (Python package, r = 0.3, after 300 burn-in steps) σ of the prey density stays at 0.13–0.18 for L = 12 … 96 in 3D, and the period is about 20 steps. In 2D at r = 0.3, and in 3D at r = 0.5, σ·Ld/2 stays constant (σ ≈ 1.7/L in 2D): only finite-size noise. In 3D, σ peaks at r ≈ 0.3 and is small below 0.2 and above 0.4, as in Fig. 8 of the paper. The figure at the end of the page shows the data.

Percolation · k-neighbour graphs k-neighbour percolation lab Directed k-neighbour graphs on ℤ², the triangular lattice and the Poisson–Delaunay triangulation, with a continuous out-degree k = 2dp and adjustable box size.

Every vertex of ℤ², of the triangular lattice or of a Poisson–Delaunay triangulation picks k of its neighbours at random and draws directed edges to them. Does the origin reach infinity along the arrows? On ℤ² the answer for k = 2 is yes: the main theorem of D. Coupier, B. Henry, B. Jahnel and J. Köppl, The planar lattice two-neighbor graph percolates, Ann. Probab., to appear, while independent directed percolation with the same density of edges does not percolate. With the continuous parametrisation k = 2dp of that paper the out-degree can be any real number.

The picture shows the forward cluster of the origin in the box [−n, n]², coloured by graph distance. Changing k keeps the sample and only adds or removes edges, so the cluster grows monotonically as k increases; “New sample” draws fresh randomness. Crossing probability

For every k on a grid of step 0.1 the lab draws independent samples and records whether the forward cluster of the origin reaches the boundary of the box Λn = [−n, n]². As n grows the curve steepens towards the percolation probability θ(k), which vanishes below the critical value kc = 2dpc and is positive above it.

Each run adds a curve for the current graph and box size (up to four are kept), so different n or graphs can be compared.

The model

Every vertex of the graph chooses, independently, k of its neighbours and draws directed edges towards them. In the continuous parametrisation of Coupier, Henry, Jahnel and Köppl, a parameter p ∈ [0, 1] on ℤd gives

k = ⌊2dp⌋, ε = 2dp − k

and each vertex picks k of its 2d neighbours uniformly at random and, with probability ε, one more among the remaining ones. Every directed edge is then open with probability p, the out-degree has mean 2dp, and the edges leaving a vertex are negatively correlated. The slider sets the mean out-degree k = 2dp directly; on ℤ² the values k = 1, 2, 3 are the k-neighbour graphs of Jahnel, Köppl, Lodewijks and Tóbiás.

The model percolates if the origin is connected to infinity by a directed open path, θ(p) = P(o ⇝ ∞) > 0. Increasing k only adds edges (the coupling used here: every vertex keeps a random order of its neighbours and opens a growing initial segment of it), so there is a critical value pc, and kc = 2dpc.

What is known

ℤ². The 1-neighbour graph does not percolate and the 3-neighbour graph does. The main theorem of the paper is that the 2-neighbour graph percolates: pc(2) < 1/2, so kc < 2. A union bound over self-avoiding paths gives pc(2) ≥ 1/c(2) > 0.373, with c(2) the connective constant, so 1.49 < kc < 2; simulations put kc near 1.84. For comparison, independent directed percolation on ℤ², with the same probability p for every edge but a random out-degree, has pc = 1/2: a fixed degree helps percolation.

Triangular lattice and Poisson–Delaunay triangulation. The same model on a different underlying graph: every vertex chooses uniformly among its 6 neighbours on the triangular lattice (k = 6p), or among its neighbours in the Delaunay triangulation of a Poisson point process of intensity 1 together with a point at the origin (mean degree 6). Only the graph changes, not the rule. Vertices with fewer than k neighbours choose all of them. The k-neighbour graphs were introduced as a discrete counterpart of the continuum k-nearest-neighbour graph of Häggström and Meester.
